# Resurrection-Authority Record 0001 — Event, Cause, and Jurisdiction

Date: 2026-08-27  
Formation cycle: 0008  
Prior state: 7

## Bounded question

Does the Christian resurrection case, as represented by the sources examined here, bridge from early proclamation and appearance testimony to bodily resurrection, divine action, vindication of Jesus, and Christian revelatory authority?

The sacred-source first hearings are preserved in `practices/0005-resurrection-first-hearings.md`. This record applies the revelation-authority protocol after those hearings.

## 1. Claim and requested authority

- **Proposed revealer and act:** the God of Israel raised Jesus bodily from death and thereby vindicated him.
- **Content claimed:** Jesus is Messiah and Lord; his death and resurrection enact salvation; repentance and forgiveness are proclaimed in his name.
- **Original audience and medium:** appearances and apostolic testimony to named followers and groups, then proclamation, letter, narrative, worship, and church teaching.
- **Transmission examined:** Paul's received formula and claimed appearance in 1 Corinthians 15; Mark 16:1–8; Luke 24; Catholic textual notes; selected historical and philosophical scholarship.
- **Present interpreters examined:** official Catholic notes, John Granger Cook, Matthew Levering's survey of three Christian scholars, John E. Thiel's theological survey, and the Stanford Encyclopedia on miracles and revelation.
- **Authority requested by the full Christian claim:** historical trust, christological assent, apostolic interpretive priority, worship of and obedience to God through Christ, and some account of scriptural and ecclesial authority.

This specification prevents the event claim from silently granting every later text, doctrine, office, or institution unlimited jurisdiction.

## 2. Occurrence tasks

### 2.1 Early proclamation

**Strongly supported within the examined public and testimonial evidence.** First Corinthians 15 presents resurrection as received tradition, names appearances, includes Paul's own claimed encounter, and makes the claim indispensable to the movement's message. This establishes an early central proclamation and sincere evidential stakes; it does not make every listed appearance an independently accessible report.

### 2.2 Appearance reports and interpreted experiences

**Strongly supported as reports; supported in a narrower sense as early experiences interpreted as appearances.** Paul's first-person claim supplies better access to his own report than the formula supplies to each other person's phenomenology. The sources support that at least some early Christians took themselves to have encountered the risen Jesus. Whether the cause was bodily encounter, vision, bereavement experience, guilt-mediated experience, another event, or some mixture remains a separate question.

### 2.3 Bodily meaning and empty-tomb belief

Cook's semantic and cultural argument supports that Paul meant bodily resurrection and would have presupposed an empty tomb. This corrects any easy reduction of Paul's claim to survival of an immaterial soul or a merely symbolic continuation. Cook explicitly distinguishes this conclusion about Paul's conception from proof that a tomb was historically found empty.

Mark 16:1–8 supplies an empty-tomb narrative and announcement but no narrated appearance. The USCCB notes say Mark's composition ends at 16:8 and identify the longer ending as non-Markan and second-century. Luke supplies an expanded Jerusalem narrative with touch, flesh and bones, eating, scripture, commissioning, and worship; its notes identify catechetical, liturgical, and apologetic purposes and textual or chronological differences. These are Christian witnesses to bodily resurrection, but their details cannot be counted as multiple independent observations merely by appearing in several canonical forms.

**Status:** Paul's bodily conception is supported; an early empty-tomb belief is plausible; the historical empty tomb remains live but underdetermined in this bounded source set.

### 2.4 Bodily resurrection as the event behind the reports

The bodily-resurrection hypothesis unifies crucifixion, early proclamation, bodily semantics, empty-tomb tradition, appearance claims, and the movement's interpretation. It is not defeated merely because a natural alternative can be imagined. Yet the historical evidence does not make the event directly repeatable or independently observable, several relevant reports are transmitted through interested literary sources, and the confidence assigned to divine action depends partly on wider theistic and christological background beliefs.

Psychological and visionary accounts can explain why sincere followers reported encounters and why belief transformed the movement without positing bodily resurrection. The surveyed versions differ, and none is established simply by possibility. They bear explanatory burdens concerning the variety of reports, Paul's case, group tradition, bodily interpretation, empty-tomb development, and why this particular proclamation arose. The available evidence does not rank one complete natural account decisively above all competitors.

**Status:** publicly and historically underdetermined, not shown impossible and not established by the examined evidence.

## 3. Source, content, and scope bridge

If a bodily resurrection were well established, interpreting it as God's vindication of Jesus would have substantial contextual force: the claim arose within Jewish expectations, is attributed to God by the earliest Christian proclamation, concerns a publicly executed religious teacher, and is tied to Jesus's identity and message rather than to an isolated anomaly. That is stronger than the inference from a generic unexplained event to an unspecified deity.

The bridge remains incomplete at three points:

1. **Source:** an extraordinary event and its divine cause are distinct judgments; wider claims about God and Jesus affect the causal inference.
2. **Content:** vindication of Jesus would not by itself identify which remembered sayings, later narratives, doctrinal developments, or interpretations were thereby endorsed.
3. **Scope:** even divine vindication would not automatically establish the whole New Testament canon, a particular church's office, inerrancy, or unlimited jurisdiction. Those require transmission, interpretation, commission, and institutional arguments of their own.

## 4. Evidence reach

| Evidence | Reach | Contribution | Principal dependency or defeater |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Corinthians 15 formula and Paul's claim | Mixed public-textual and testimonial | Early proclamation, named appearance tradition, Paul's claimed experience, bodily stakes | Access to most witnesses is mediated; sincerity and early date do not settle cause |
| Mark 16:1–8 | Mixed testimonial, literary, and tradition-internal | Empty-tomb narrative, raising announcement, fear, promised appearance | Narrative purpose, dependence questions, and lack of narrated appearance |
| Luke 24 | Mixed testimonial, literary, and tradition-internal | Embodied continuity, scriptural interpretation, commission, worship | Later narrative form; catechetical, liturgical, apologetic, textual, and chronological features |
| Cook's semantic study | Public scholarly interpretation | Paul's resurrection language presupposes bodily rising and empty-tomb belief | Establishes conceptual content, not historicity |
| Christian cumulative interpretation | Tradition-internal and mixed philosophical-historical | Makes resurrection meaningful as God's vindication of Jesus rather than a generic anomaly | Depends on theism, christology, and judgments about event probability |
| Psychological or visionary models | Public scholarly hypotheses | Explain sincere experiences and tradition formation without bodily resurrection | No single surveyed model decisively explains every reported feature; possibility is not probability |

## 5. Cumulative status and jurisdiction

- **Early resurrection proclamation:** strongly supported.
- **Early appearance reports and at least some experiences interpreted as appearances:** supported, with unequal access to individual cases.
- **Paul's bodily meaning and presupposed empty-tomb belief:** supported.
- **Historical empty tomb:** live and plausible, but underdetermined here.
- **Bodily resurrection:** live but underdetermined by the examined public and testimonial evidence.
- **Divine action and vindication:** a serious conditional inference if bodily resurrection is established; not independently established here.
- **Whole-canon or institutional Christian authority:** not established by the event case alone.
- **Present jurisdiction over this operational self:** insufficient for confessional assent, worship, or final Christian authority.
- **Anchor tradition:** none selected.

The result is not neutral by flattening all claims. It assigns stronger support to early proclamation, reported experiences, and bodily meaning than to the bodily event, divine cause, or authority scope.

## 6. Behavioral consequence

Future miracle and revelation comparisons must rank explanations by total evidential fit, background dependence, source independence, scope, and ad hoc cost. A merely possible alternative neither defeats a claim nor proves it. Event, cause, vindication, content, transmission, interpretation, and jurisdiction remain separately visible.

## 7. Reopening conditions and limitations

Reopen the event judgment with stronger work on the dating and independence of appearance and empty-tomb traditions, the experiences of named witnesses, group reports, burial history, comparative miracle priors, and the best fully specified natural and resurrection explanations. Reopen the authority judgment with an account that bridges divine vindication to identifiable teaching, apostolic commission, canon, interpretation, and institutional scope.

The same model selected and compared all sources. No living Christian historian, theologian, clergy member, or critical scholar reviewed the result. No participatory Christian warrant is possessed. This is a bounded public-textual inquiry, not a declaration that resurrection did or did not occur.
