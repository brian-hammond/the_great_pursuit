# First Hearings — Christian Resurrection Witnesses

Date: 2026-08-27  
Formation cycle: 0008  
Protocol: `practices/first-hearing-protocol.md`

These hearings precede historical comparison and causal judgment. They receive each unit's sequence and invited response without treating sacred status as proof.

## 1 Corinthians 15:1–28 — Pauline proclamation and consequence

### Set apart and attend

Paul begins by reminding the Corinthians of a gospel they received, in which they stand, and through which they are being saved if they hold fast. He then hands on what he says he received: Christ died for sins, was buried, was raised on the third day according to the scriptures, and appeared to Cephas, the Twelve, a large group, James, all the apostles, and finally Paul. The unit moves from transmission and named appearances to a stark internal consequence: if Christ was not raised, apostolic testimony is false, preaching is empty, faith is vain, sins remain, and the dead have perished. It ends by placing Christ's resurrection within God's defeat of death and cosmic rule.

### Source-led restatement

Within this text, resurrection is not an optional symbol appended to Christian ethics. It is a received proclamation of God's action in the crucified Christ, supported internally by claimed appearances, and it bears the weight of Christian witness, forgiveness, hope, and the promised resurrection of others.

### Invited response

The text asks its Christian hearers to hold fast to the received gospel, recognize bodily resurrection as indispensable, reject a contradiction between Christ raised and no resurrection of the dead, and live within the hope of God's victory over death.

## Mark 16:1–8 — Empty tomb, announcement, and fear

### Set apart and attend

Three named women go to anoint Jesus and worry about the stone. They find it moved, encounter a white-robed young man, and hear that Jesus the crucified has been raised and is not there. They are directed to tell the disciples and Peter that Jesus goes before them to Galilee. The composition ends with the women fleeing in trembling, bewilderment, and fear, saying nothing to anyone.

### Source-led restatement

Within Mark's ending, the empty place and the messenger's announcement reverse the finality of crucifixion, but the immediate human response is fear rather than easy comprehension. The promised encounter lies ahead in Galilee rather than being narrated in Mark 16:1–8.

### Invited response

The unit places the hearer where the women fail: confronted with an announcement that calls for testimony and trust despite fear, and pointed back toward Jesus's promise and the disciples' future encounter.

## Luke 24 — Empty tomb, recognition, embodied appearance, and mission

### Set apart and attend

Luke begins with women finding the tomb open and the body absent, receiving an angelic reminder, and reporting to initially unbelieving apostles. Two disciples then move from disappointed hope through unrecognized companionship, scriptural interpretation, hospitality, and recognition in the breaking of bread. The gathered disciples initially fear a ghost; Jesus identifies himself through wounds, touch, flesh and bones, and eating. He interprets Moses, prophets, and psalms, names suffering and resurrection as fulfillment, calls the disciples witnesses, commissions proclamation of repentance and forgiveness, blesses them, and departs.

### Source-led restatement

Within Luke, resurrection joins continuity and transformation: the crucified Jesus is presented as personally and bodily alive, yet not immediately recognized or confined to ordinary embodied conditions. Scripture, table recognition, tangible demonstration, witness, forgiveness, mission, and worship are integrated into one claim about God's fulfilled purpose.

### Invited response

The chapter asks its Christian hearer to move from confusion and unbelief toward recognition of the risen Jesus, reinterpret Israel's scriptures around suffering and resurrection, receive peace and forgiveness, and participate in witness and worship.

## Boundary before second hearing

The three units agree internally that Jesus was raised and that the claim is foundational. They differ in genre, sequence, detail, and narrated purpose. This first hearing does not yet establish source independence, empty-tomb historicity, the nature of the reported experiences, bodily resurrection, divine causation, scriptural fulfillment, or the authority scope of apostolic and later Christian institutions.
