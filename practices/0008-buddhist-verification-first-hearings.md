# First Hearings — Inquiry, Teacher Scrutiny, and Realization

Date: 2026-08-27  
Formation cycle: 0011  
Protocol: `practices/first-hearing-protocol.md`

These hearings receive three early Buddhist discourses in their sequence before using them as generic epistemology or comparing them with clinical research. They are read in English translation without Pali philological expertise, Buddhist initiation, meditation experience, or lineage standing.

## Aṅguttara Nikāya 3.65 — The Kālāmas at Kesamutta

### Set apart and attend

The people of Kesamutta are confused because visiting ascetics and brahmins promote their own teachings while denigrating rivals. The Buddha first recognizes that their doubt concerns a doubtful matter. He tells them not to settle it merely by oral transmission, lineage, report, canonical collection, logic, inference, reflection, acceptance of a considered view, apparent competence, or the thought that an ascetic is their teacher. The discourse then turns to greed, hate, and delusion: the Kālāmas examine whether these lead to killing, theft, sexual misconduct, falsehood, harm, and suffering, and whether their opposites lead to welfare and happiness. The appraisal is both personal and social: what they know in enactment is also blameworthy or blameless and criticized or praised by sensible people. Loving-kindness, compassion, appreciative joy, and equanimity culminate in four assurances that preserve present ethical benefit across alternative judgments about afterlife and karmic result. The Kālāmas end by taking refuge in Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha.

### Source-led restatement

Within this unit, doubt is not answered by swapping one prestigious assertion for another. The audience is guided through the observable fruits of greed, hate, delusion, and their opposites, with personal knowledge joined to ethical consequence and the judgment of the wise. The discourse does not present inquiry as preference without discipline, and its ending moves from examination to confidence and refuge.

### Invited response

The unit asks hearers to refuse unexamined deference, attend to the enacted causes of harm and welfare, cultivate non-greed, non-hate, and understanding, and let ethical testing become a basis for committed practice.

## Majjhima Nikāya 47 — The Inquirer

### Set apart and attend

The Buddha instructs an inquiring mendicant who cannot read another's mind to scrutinize the Tathāgata through what can be seen and heard. The inquiry asks whether corrupt, mixed, or clean qualities appear; whether the good state is longstanding; whether fame and renown disclose new dangers; and whether restraint is secure rather than forced. The inquirer observes conduct across community and solitude, asks the teacher directly, approaches to hear a teaching given in higher and lower stages and in dark and bright aspects, and directly knows a principle in accordance with the teaching. This graded encounter grounds confidence that the Buddha is awakened, the Dhamma well explained, and the Saṅgha practicing well.

### Source-led restatement

Within this discourse, confidence is neither immediate charisma nor solitary self-certification. Visible and audible conduct, duration, pressure, questioning, graded instruction, and a measure of direct knowledge form a path toward confidence. The disciple cannot encompass the teacher's mind, so the inquiry uses accessible signs without pretending that observation and terminal awakening are identical.

### Invited response

The discourse asks a disciple to investigate character patiently, test stability under status and circumstance, listen closely, learn by stages, and let directly known teaching inform—not replace—the wider judgment of teacher and community.

## Majjhima Nikāya 95 — With Caṅkī

### Set apart and attend

In discussion with the brahmin student Kāpaṭhika, the Buddha distinguishes preserving truth, awakening to truth, and attaining truth. Faith, endorsement, oral transmission, reasoning, and a deliberated view may each turn out true or false; a truth-preserving person reports the actual basis—“such is my faith” or “such is the view I accepted”—without declaring that alone to be the only truth. Discovery begins by scrutinizing a teacher for greed, hate, and delusion that might produce false claims or harmful instruction. Confidence then leads to approach, respect, listening, remembering, examination of meaning, reflective acceptance, enthusiasm, effort, weighing, striving, and direct realization. Repeated cultivation and development, rather than the first glimpse alone, lead to attainment.

### Source-led restatement

Within this unit, epistemic honesty names the present basis of confidence without confusing it with realization. Teacher scrutiny, receptive learning, deliberation, exertion, personal realization, and sustained cultivation are ordered but non-interchangeable. Neither inherited testimony nor one insight is treated as the finished path.

### Invited response

The discourse asks hearers to preserve truth in speech, investigate the teacher's motives and conduct, accept the labor and sequence of training, and refrain from claiming arrival when they possess only confidence, transmission, reasoning, or an initial discovery.

## Consequence of delaying analysis

The first hearing defeats two flattenings. Early Buddhist inquiry is not blind submission, but it is also not an instant, purely private laboratory test of every Buddhist doctrine. These texts describe a hybrid path of ethical observation, wise appraisal, teacher scrutiny, provisional confidence, graded training, direct realization, and repeated cultivation. Later analysis must therefore state which rung a piece of evidence reaches instead of calling the entire path either publicly verified or evidentially empty.
