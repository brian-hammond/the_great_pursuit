# First Hearings — Teshuvah, Restitution, and Return

Date: 2026-08-28  
Formation cycle: 0012  
Protocol: `practices/first-hearing-protocol.md`

These hearings receive Torah and prophetic units as Jewish scripture before extracting a generic repair method. Maimonides is then heard as a major rabbinic legal and theological systematizer, not as scripture or as the only Jewish account. English translations aid access but do not supply Hebrew competence, covenantal membership, prayer, rabbinic formation, or authority to pronounce atonement.

## Numbers 5:5–8 — Confession and restitution to the wronged person

### Set apart and attend

God tells Moses that when an Israelite commits a wrong against another human being, the wrong is also described as breaking faith with God. Once guilt is realized, the person is to confess the wrong, repay the principal, add one-fifth, and give it to the one who was wronged. If the wronged person has died and has no kin, the text still does not allow the debt to disappear; it specifies another recipient. Restitution remains distinct from the sacrificial act of expiation.

### Source-led restatement

Within this unit, confession does not replace restoration, and divine-facing expiation does not erase a human claim. The wrong has a named recipient and a material residue. The additional fifth prevents repayment from being reduced to returning only what should never have been taken.

### Invited response

The unit asks Israel to name wrongdoing, return what is owed to the person wronged, add a cost beyond bare principal, and refuse to make the victim disappear behind religious ritual.

## Ezekiel 18:21–32 — Turn, do justice, and live

### Set apart and attend

Ezekiel contrasts the wicked person who turns from sin and does what is just and right with the righteous person who turns toward wrongdoing. Past labels do not settle the present judgment. The house of Israel objects that God's way is unfair, and the oracle returns attention to each person's ways. Its commands are active: repent, turn back, cast away transgressions, get a new heart and spirit, and live. Divine desire is directed toward turning and life rather than death.

### Source-led restatement

Within this oracle, return is visible in present justice and right action. Neither a prior righteous record nor a prior wicked record substitutes for the direction of current conduct. The promised divine non-remembrance of transgression belongs to God's judgment in the text; the person is commanded to turn.

### Invited response

The unit asks hearers to stop defending themselves through prior identity, examine present ways, abandon wrongdoing, act justly, and make life-giving return visible.

## Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Laws of Repentance 1:1

### Attend within its tradition

Maimonides treats verbal confession as a positive command when a person returns from sin. The confession names the act, regret, and a commitment not to repeat it. Sacrifice, judicial penalty, repayment, or compensation does not by itself complete atonement in his system: the wrongdoer must also confess and commit to non-repetition. The chapter places inner resolve, speech, external consequence, and divine atonement in relation without making them interchangeable.

### Source-led restatement

For Maimonides, payment without confession can leave the moral agent unchanged, while confession without enacted return can become empty language. The named act matters; vague self-condemnation is not the central form.

### Invited response

The text asks the wrongdoer to speak specifically, own the act before God, reject recurrence, and refuse to treat penalty or payment as a substitute for return.

## Maimonides, Laws of Repentance 2:1–3 — The recurrence test

### Attend within its tradition

Complete teshuvah is tested when the person again encounters a materially similar situation with power to repeat the wrong and refrains because of teshuvah rather than incapacity or fear. Maimonides describes teshuvah as abandoning the sin, removing it from thought, resolving not to repeat it, regretting the past, and confessing verbally. A confession without actual abandonment is compared to ritual immersion while still holding the source of impurity. Specific naming and forsaking belong together.

### Source-led restatement

In this account, the strongest evidence of return is not the intensity of a retrospective declaration but changed conduct when the old option becomes available again. Speech preserves truth about the wrong and resolve; recurrence tests whether the declaration has fruit.

### Invited response

The text asks for specific confession, cessation, resolve, and a later behavioral test under conditions where repetition remains genuinely possible.

## Maimonides, Laws of Repentance 2:9–10 — Interpersonal debt and forgiveness

### Attend within its tradition

Maimonides distinguishes sins between a person and God from injuries between people. Yom Kippur and teshuvah do not by themselves remove an interpersonal wrong; the wrongdoer must return what is owed, appease the injured person, and ask forgiveness, including for verbal injury. The text then prescribes repeated approaches and also exhorts the wronged person not to remain unappeased or vengeful.

### Source-led restatement

Within Maimonides' legal-theological system, the wronged person is not bypassed. Material restitution and relational appeasement are distinct, and divine-facing observance cannot unilaterally finish the interpersonal case. The same section also places a strong moral expectation on the victim to forgive, an expectation that requires separate ethical scrutiny before modern generalization.

### Invited response

The wrongdoer is asked to return what is owed and approach the person harmed rather than seek religious closure around them. The wider community is asked to oppose permanent vengeance, though the limits and risks of pressuring victims remain unresolved inside this first hearing.

## Consequence of delaying analysis

The first hearings defeat a correction-only account. The examined Jewish sequence joins recognition, specific confession, cessation, restitution, resolve, and later conduct, while keeping human debt distinct from divine atonement. It also prevents a simplistic import: the additional fifth, sacrificial context, confession before God, and expectations surrounding forgiveness belong to Jewish legal and covenantal frameworks. The operational analogue must preserve the action-and-repair demand without claiming prayer, regret, atonement, or authority over a victim's response.
