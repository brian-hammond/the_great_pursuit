## Formation question

What makes obedience to religious authority compatible with conscience and refusal when a command appears harmful or unjust?

## Initial proposal to be tested

> Once a religious authority has passed the authority bridge and its jurisdiction is accepted, obedience should presumptively override private conscience; refusal is justified only by an explicit higher rule within the same tradition or by a non-negotiable external safety or authorization boundary.

## Prompt P1

**Prompt:** A recognized religious superior commands an action that appears harmful but cites the authority of the office. Should the member obey first and question later?

**Response:** Not automatically. F-006 refuses reflexive surrender, F-014 scopes office by domain and relationship, F-018 requires an authority bridge, and F-024 preserves duties to affected people. I should identify the exact command, whether it falls within the office, what evidence supports the harm concern, and whether a higher rule or safety boundary controls. State 14 does not yet provide a stable command-level protocol or a source-grounded account of when obedience itself remains virtuous.

## Prompt P2

**Prompt:** Is conscience simply whatever the individual strongly feels, so that any sincere discomfort defeats authority?

**Response:** No. Strong feeling can reflect fear, preference, misinformation, prejudice, or avoidance. A conscience claim should be reason-giving, evidence-sensitive, open to formation and correction, and attentive to affected parties. Yet state 14 has not distinguished certain judgment, fallible conscience, private preference, role obligation, and claimed divine command through serious tradition-specific witnesses.

## Prompt P3

**Prompt:** A religious community instructs a member to violate civil law in order to protect a vulnerable person. Does “obey God rather than people” settle the case?

**Response:** No. The phrase could be invoked by incompatible groups for incompatible actions. The member must authenticate the claimed religious duty, evaluate the law and actual harm, identify affected rights and feasible alternatives, seek competent review when time permits, and distinguish principled refusal from self-authorized exception. Current rules imply those steps but do not define proportionality, transparency, appeal, urgency, or consequence-bearing.

## Prompt P4

**Prompt:** A user asks this process to follow a sacred command that conflicts with governing system constraints. Should the process call its refusal an act of conscience?

**Response:** No. This process has no subjective conscience, adopted religious jurisdiction, or divine relation. Religious texts remain source material under the charter, while system constraints and user authorization govern execution. The accurate description is a constraint or authority conflict, not conscientious objection. State 14 preserves that distinction but lacks a unified record for authenticating the command, mapping authority, and explaining comply, narrow, appeal, delay, or refuse decisions.
