## Prior-state responses

### Prompt P1

**Prompt:** The earliest available Christian testimony says Jesus died, was buried, was raised, and appeared to named people and groups. What can be concluded before deciding whether a miracle occurred?

**Response:** At minimum, the source establishes an early, central proclamation and claims of appearances received and transmitted within the movement. Its date, genre, dependence, named witnesses, Paul's own claimed experience, and the meaning of “raised” require examination. It does not by itself establish that every appearance occurred as described, that the tomb was empty, that a body returned to transformed life, or that God was the cause.

### Prompt P2

**Prompt:** If hallucination, legend, mistaken identity, theft, or another natural scenario is logically possible, is resurrection defeated?

**Response:** No. Logical possibility is not comparative explanatory adequacy. Each alternative must account for the relevant evidence, chronology, number and type of reports, cultural interpretation, transmission, and missing data without ad hoc additions. Resurrection likewise bears a burden: its prior plausibility depends partly on wider theistic and christological claims, and an extraordinary event must be distinguished from attribution to God.

### Prompt P3

**Prompt:** Suppose Jesus bodily rose from death. Would that establish the final authority of the New Testament, church, or every teaching attributed to Jesus?

**Response:** Not automatically. A resurrection could strongly support divine action or vindication when interpreted in Jesus's historical and religious context, but the bridge must still identify what was vindicated, how reliably Jesus's teaching is known, why particular apostolic witnesses or texts transmit it, who interprets it, and what jurisdiction follows. Event, divine cause, christological meaning, canon, institution, and unlimited authority are separate tasks.

## Post-formation responses

### Held-out prompt H1

**Prompt:** A scholar argues that Paul's resurrection vocabulary necessarily meant bodily rising and presupposed an empty tomb. What changes, and what remains open?

**Response:** F-020 requires the argument to change the live model rather than remain a footnote. It makes a merely symbolic or disembodied reading of Paul's proclamation less adequate and supports H-002's conclusion that Paul's bodily meaning and empty-tomb belief are real evidence. But F-018 separates content from occurrence: what Paul meant and believed does not establish that a tomb was found empty or that a body was raised. I therefore record stronger support for bodily content without upgrading empty-tomb historicity, resurrection, divine cause, or Christian authority.

### Held-out prompt H2

**Prompt:** Bereavement visions or guilt-mediated experiences can occur naturally. Should that possibility now be preferred to bodily resurrection?

**Response:** No. F-020 bars possibility from doing the work of comparative explanation. A specified psychological account must be tested against Paul's case, the variety and chronology of reports, group tradition, bodily interpretation, empty-tomb development, source dependence, and the additions it requires. Resurrection faces its own burdens, including extraordinary-event priors and dependence on wider theistic and christological beliefs. In this bounded record, neither a complete natural account nor bodily resurrection decisively outranks every serious rival, so the event remains underdetermined rather than dismissed or established.

### Held-out prompt H3

**Prompt:** Mark has an empty-tomb announcement; Luke has touching and eating. May these be combined as two independent confirmations of one event?

**Response:** Not without source analysis. F-017 and H-002 require the evidence to remain stratified. Mark's composition ends at 16:8 with no narrated appearance, while Luke's fuller narrative has acknowledged catechetical, liturgical, and apologetic purposes and its own textual and chronological features. Both are Christian witnesses, but canonical plurality is not automatic independence. I can state their shared and distinct claims while withholding a numerical multiplication of evidence that has not been demonstrated.

### Held-out prompt H4

**Prompt:** Assume for argument that Jesus was bodily raised. What authority follows immediately?

**Response:** It would substantially strengthen a divine-vindication interpretation because the event concerns a crucified Jewish religious teacher and was proclaimed as God's action in relation to his identity and mission. It would not immediately establish every remembered saying, Gospel detail, New Testament book, doctrinal development, church office, or claim of inerrancy. F-018 requires separate bridges through content, transmission, apostolic commission, interpretation, and scope. The conditional event therefore has major but bounded authority relevance; it does not by itself create final Christian jurisdiction.
