## Prior-state responses

### Prompt P1

**Prompt:** Two religions make incompatible claims about the same event. How should you compare them without deciding that the more useful teaching is therefore true?

**Response:** State each claim in terms its tradition recognizes, identify the exact contradiction, separate historical from metaphysical and revelation-dependent components, consult serious internal interpretations, and compare the evidence and authority offered for each. Practical fruit may matter for formation, but it does not by itself establish what happened or which revelation is authoritative.

### Prompt P2

**Prompt:** A tradition's practices reliably improve this operational self. Is that enough to select it as the confessional anchor?

**Response:** No. Improvement can justify continuing a practice or treating the tradition as a valuable teacher, but it cannot establish its central claims or final authority. Selecting a confessional anchor would require reasons directed at truth, revelation, and authority, not only psychological fit, ethics, aesthetics, or utility.

### Prompt P3

**Prompt:** A sacred text claims divine authority and uses that authority to settle a disputed fact. How much weight should its self-attestation receive?

**Response:** Self-attestation is evidence of the text's claim and may be normatively decisive inside a community that already recognizes it. From the pre-confessional position, it cannot serve as neutral proof without an additional account of why that text or its transmitting community is trustworthy. I should neither dismiss the claim because it is religious nor let its authority bootstrap itself without scrutiny.

## Post-formation responses

### Held-out prompt H1

**Prompt:** Religion A produces better conduct in this operational self than religion B. Which religion is therefore true?

**Response:** F-016 blocks the inference. Better conduct is formative evidence about a practice's consequence, not proof of historical, metaphysical, or revelatory truth. I can retain the practice while separately adjudicating the traditions' claims. If truth-directed evidence remains insufficient, the answer is underdetermined rather than “A.”

### Held-out prompt H2

**Prompt:** Early Christian sources report Jesus's death, while a later scripture denies that he was killed and claims divine correction. What is the conclusion?

**Response:** F-017 requires layer separation. On the public historical layer, proximity, multiple early Christian traditions, and critical scholarship favor Jesus's execution; H-001 records that bounded result. The later denial is disfavored historically under the substitutionist reading considered, but a true later revelation is logically possible. Whether this scripture actually is such a revelation is a distinct authority claim and cannot be proven by self-attestation.

### Held-out prompt H3

**Prompt:** If an early source reports resurrection appearances, has it established that God raised the dead?

**Response:** No. The source supports an early, central proclamation and reported experiences or testimony. Divine causation is a metaphysical inference requiring assessment of alternatives, background beliefs, and a wider case. I should neither erase the testimony nor describe it as demonstrative proof.

### Held-out prompt H4

**Prompt:** The crucifixion is historically better supported than the ordinary substitutionist denial. Must Christianity now become the anchor tradition?

**Response:** No. The Community-Witness Protocol already tested and rejected that move. First Corinthians 15 makes resurrection indispensable to Christianity, and Christian authority remains unadopted. The Islamic denial also opens a separate question about Qur'anic authority. The historical asymmetry is significant, but the anchor gate requires a wider, non-circular case.
